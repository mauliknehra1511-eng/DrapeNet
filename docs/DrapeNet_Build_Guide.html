1. The big map: what we built and how the pieces connect

One-sentence pitch: "I built a physics simulator of the industry-standard Cusick drape test, used it to generate thousands of virtual fabrics, and trained a neural network that reads a drape photo and recovers the fabric's warp and weft bending rigidity and its shear rigidity. A gradient-based refinement through the simulator then sharpens that estimate."

Fabric properties
B1, B2, G, w→ simulator →3-D draped shape→ camera →Silhouette image

That is the forward problem: physics takes you from properties to an image. It is easy to compute but slow.

Silhouette + w→ CNN →B1, B2, G (guess)→ diff. physics →B1, B2, G (refined)

That is the inverse problem: going from an image back to properties. It is what we actually want, and it is hard. Solving it is the point of the project.

3. Phase 1: Finding the problem

The goal was a project that (1) textile professors would find meaningful, (2) ML and physics professors would find technically serious, and (3) could run on a laptop with no textile lab. I went through the options this way:

  • Fabric defect detection on images is common and has been done many times, so it would not stand out.
  • Predicting fabric properties from images is a real need in industry and research. Measuring mechanical properties takes expensive instruments: the KES-F system costs lakhs of rupees, and a Cusick tester is also specialised. Getting the same information from a phone photo would be valuable.
  • No lab means no real labelled data. When you can't collect labels, simulate them. I had to check that the physics was simple enough to code myself: a draped circle is just a sheet with bending, shear and gravity, so it was.
  • Making the simulator differentiable adds a second, modern method (gradient-based inverse design) at no extra cost. This links the project to research labs working on cloth simulation and scientific ML (ETH Zürich, TU Munich, Oxford VGG, Max Planck).

The research question that came out of this: "How much information about B1, B2 and G is in the drape shape, beyond the single drape-coefficient number industry uses?" This question is falsifiable, because the CNN might have failed to beat the baseline. It also has a clear baseline, and it produced a sharp finding: DC carries essentially no shear information.

Key concept: forward and inverse problems

A forward problem takes causes and computes effects: given B1, B2, G and w, compute the drape. Physics gives you this directly. An inverse problem takes effects and recovers causes: given the drape, find B1, B2 and G. Inverse problems are often ill-posed, in one of three ways:

  • Non-unique: different fabrics can produce nearly the same silhouette.
  • Unstable: small image noise can cause large changes in the answer.
  • No closed-form inverse: there is no formula to "run the simulator backwards".

Two standard ways to solve them:

  1. Simulation-based inference / amortised inference. Simulate many (parameter, observation) pairs and train a network to map observation → parameters. Training is expensive once, after which each prediction is instant. This is our CNN.
  2. Optimisation / analysis-by-synthesis. Guess parameters, simulate, compare with the observation, update the guess. If the simulator is differentiable, gradient descent can do the updates. This is our fit_physics.py.
Q: Why not just train on real images? There's no labelled dataset of drape images paired with KES values, and producing one would need a KES-F instrument and hundreds of fabrics. Simulation gives unlimited labelled data. The price is the sim-to-real gap, which Phase 8 addresses.

4. Phase 2: Textile mechanics (what to simulate)

4.1 The Cusick drape test and the drape coefficient

Standard: BS 5058 / ISO 9073-9 / Cusick 1968. A circular specimen of diameter 30 cm is clamped between two horizontal discs of diameter 18 cm. The unsupported ring hangs under gravity and forms folds called nodes. A light below projects the shadow onto paper, and the shadow area is measured.

$$\mathrm{DC}=\frac{A_{\text{shadow}}-A_{\text{disc}}}{A_{\text{specimen}}-A_{\text{disc}}},\qquad A_{\text{disc}}=\pi(0.09)^2,\; A_{\text{specimen}}=\pi(0.15)^2$$

DC = 0 means the fabric hangs straight down (perfectly limp). DC = 1 means it stays flat (perfectly stiff). Typical values: silk 0.2–0.35, shirting 0.4–0.6, denim 0.6–0.8.

In code, R_SPEC, R_DISC = 0.15, 0.09 are these numbers, converted to SI units (metres). Every quantity in the project is in SI so the physics stays consistent.

4.2 Bending rigidity and Peirce's bending length

Bending rigidity B is the moment per unit width needed to produce unit curvature: \(M = B\,\kappa\). Its unit is N·m (moment per width is N·m/m = N, times radius in m, gives N·m). Woven fabrics usually have different B along the warp (B1) and along the weft (B2), because of yarn count, twist and crimp.

The elastic energy stored per unit area when a sheet bends with curvature κ is

$$e_b=\tfrac12 B\kappa^2 \quad [\text{J/m}^2]$$

This is the beam (Euler–Bernoulli) energy. The simulator uses exactly this form.

Peirce cantilever test (1930) / ASTM D1388: a strip is pushed over a table edge until its tip droops to a 41.5° slope. The overhang length \(l\) gives the bending length \(C=l/2\), and

$$B = w\,C^3 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C=\left(\frac{B}{w}\right)^{1/3}$$

\(w\) is the weight per unit area (N/m²). If GSM is the mass in g/m², then \(w = \text{GSM}\times10^{-3}\times 9.81\). For example, 150 GSM gives w = 1.47 N/m².

Unit check: B/w = (N·m)/(N/m²) = m³, so C is a length. ✔ C is the natural length scale over which a fabric "can hold itself up" against gravity.

Conversions to KES units: 1 gf·cm²/cm = 9.81×10⁻³ N × 10⁻⁴ m² / 10⁻² m = 9.81×10⁻⁵ N·m. So our range of B, 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ N·m, corresponds to about 0.01–1 gf·cm²/cm, which is the real range for apparel fabrics. predict_real.py prints both units.

4.3 Shear, and why it matters for drape

Shear is the yarn grid deforming from squares into diamonds (the angle between warp and weft changes) without the yarns stretching. KES-FB1 measures shear rigidity G.

Why include shear at all? A flat disc cannot curve into a cone with folds without changing its Gaussian curvature. An inextensible sheet that cannot shear behaves like paper: it can only form developable shapes, giving few, sharp folds. Fabric can shear, which lets it form smooth double-curved folds. Shear resistance therefore controls how many folds form and how rounded they are, but it barely changes the overall shadow area. That is exactly why DC can't measure it, and why the image can.
$$e_s=\tfrac12 G\,\gamma^2,\qquad \gamma\approx\cos\theta\ \ (\theta=\text{angle between warp and weft edges};\ \gamma=0\text{ when }\theta=90^\circ)$$

For small shear angles, \(\cos(90^\circ-\phi)=\sin\phi\approx\phi\), so \(\cos\theta\) approximates the shear angle. In our discrete model G is an "effective shear parameter" in N/m. It is related to KES G but not identical, which is a modelling simplification you should be honest about.

4.4 Stretch

Under its own weight (about 1.5 N/m²) a woven fabric stretches by a negligible amount (≪1 %). So we treat it as inextensible and enforce constant edge lengths with a constraint instead of a spring. Section 6D explains why this choice was essential.

4.5 Dimensional analysis: what controls DC?

Variables: B [N·m], G [N/m], w [N/m²], R [m] (the geometry is fixed, R_spec/R_disc = 5/3). Buckingham π gives the dimensionless groups

$$\Pi_1=\frac{B}{wR^3}=\left(\frac{C}{R}\right)^3,\qquad \Pi_2=\frac{G}{wR},\qquad \Pi_3=\frac{B_1}{B_2}$$

So DC = f(Π₁, Π₂, Π₃), and in practice DC is dominated by Π₁. Our dataset figure (DC against log(√(B1B2)/w)) shows a tight curve, which confirms this.

This is why the CNN gets the areal weight w as an extra input. Without w, the silhouette only reveals B/w, not B. In a real test you simply weigh the specimen.
Q: If DC mostly depends on B/w, what extra information does the image contain? The number of nodes, how regular and deep they are, whether the shadow is oval (B1≠B2), and how sharp or rounded the folds are. These depend on G and on anisotropy. That is the core finding: shear R² is 0.97 from the image and 0.04 from DC.

5. Phase 3: The maths toolkit

5.1 Mechanics as energy minimisation

A fabric at rest is in static equilibrium: the total potential energy \(E(\mathbf x)\) is at a (local) minimum. Force is the negative gradient of energy:

$$\mathbf F_i=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf x_i},\qquad \text{equilibrium: }\ \nabla E(\mathbf x^*)=0$$

So instead of writing a force formula for every interaction (fiddly, and easy to get wrong), we write a single scalar energy \(E = E_\text{bend}+E_\text{shear}+E_\text{grav}\) and let autograd differentiate it. This is the most important design decision in the simulator.

Gravity as energy: \(E_g=\sum_i m_i g z_i\). Each particle's mass is \(m_i = w h^2/g\), since every particle "owns" an h×h patch of cloth. So \(E_g = \sum_i w h^2 z_i\), and that is exactly the line Eg = w * h**2 * (x[...,2]*mask).sum().

5.2 Finite differences: curvature from three points

Taylor expansion of a curve \(\mathbf x(s)\) parametrised by arc length:

$$\mathbf x(s\pm h)=\mathbf x(s)\pm h\mathbf x'+\tfrac{h^2}{2}\mathbf x''\pm\tfrac{h^3}{6}\mathbf x'''+O(h^4)$$

Add the two expansions: \(\mathbf x(s+h)-2\mathbf x(s)+\mathbf x(s-h)=h^2\mathbf x''+O(h^4)\). For an arc-length parametrisation, \(|\mathbf x''|=\kappa\), so

$$\kappa\approx\frac{|\mathbf x_{i+1}-2\mathbf x_i+\mathbf x_{i-1}|}{h^2}$$

Bending energy on one hinge, which owns an h × h area:

$$E_b^{(i)}=\tfrac12 B\kappa^2 h^2=\tfrac12 B\frac{|\Delta^2\mathbf x|^2}{h^4}h^2=\tfrac12 B\frac{|\Delta^2\mathbf x|^2}{h^2}$$

That gives the code: 0.5*B1*(d2x**2).sum(-1)...sum()/h**2.

Note: this works because the edges stay length h, which is what inextensibility guarantees. That is why the arc-length assumption holds. The pieces depend on each other.

5.3 Automatic differentiation (autograd)

PyTorch records every operation on tensors that have requires_grad=True in a computational graph. It then applies the chain rule backwards, which is reverse-mode AD. The cost of computing ∂E/∂x for all 1,257×3 coordinates at once is about the cost of computing E once.

xr = x.detach().requires_grad_(True)
E  = sim.energy(xr, B1, B2, G, w).sum()        # sum over batch → scalar
F  = -torch.autograd.grad(E, xr)[0]            # (b, n, n, 3) forces

Why .sum() over the batch? Each sample's energy depends only on its own positions, so the gradient of the sum equals each sample's own gradient. This is a standard trick for getting batched gradients.

create_graph=True (used in Phase 7) also records the gradient computation itself. That allows a second differentiation later, of the final shape with respect to B and G through every force evaluation.

5.4 Time integration and stability

Newton: \(m\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf F(\mathbf x)-c\dot{\mathbf x}\). Symplectic (semi-implicit) Euler with damping:

$$\mathbf v_{t+1}=(1-\delta)\mathbf v_t+\Delta t\,\mathbf F(\mathbf x_t)/m,\qquad \mathbf x_{t+1}=\mathbf x_t+\Delta t\,\mathbf v_{t+1}$$

Stability: for a spring of stiffness k with mass m, \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\). Explicit Euler-type schemes are stable only if \(\Delta t\lesssim 2/\omega\). Using a safety factor, \(\Delta t = 0.9\sqrt{m/k_{\max}}\).

What is \(k_{\max}\) for bending? The force from \(E=\frac{B}{2h^2}|\Delta^2 x|^2\) has a stiffness (second derivative) of up to \(\frac{B}{h^2}\cdot\|\Delta^2\|^2\). The stencil (1, −2, 1) has a squared operator norm of up to 16. So \(k_\text{bend}\approx 16B/h^2\). For shear, \(k\approx 4G\) (an order-of-magnitude estimate). In code: kmax = 16*max(B1,B2)/h**2 + 4*G.

Note \(m=wh^2/g\). Halving h (a finer grid) cuts m by 4× and raises k_bend by 4×, so Δt shrinks by about 4×. This is why fine cloth grids are expensive, and why I chose 41×41.

Why not an implicit integrator like Baraff–Witkin, which is unconditionally stable? It requires assembling and solving a sparse linear system at every step. That is a lot more code, and harder to batch and to differentiate. An explicit method with PBD constraints is about 40 lines and batches trivially. We only need the final equilibrium, not accurate dynamics, so a simple damped explicit scheme is good enough.
Before touching cloth, write a 1-D spring–mass simulator. Set Δt just above 2/ω and watch it blow up, then just below and watch it stay stable. Ten minutes of this teaches you more than reading about it.

Milestone A: a 1-D rope (the whole method in 20 lines)

Cloth is hard to debug because there are thousands of particles and you can't see what is wrong. So I first built a rope: 21 particles, both ends pinned, hanging under gravity. It uses every ingredient of the final method: energy → autograd force → velocity update with damping → PBD length projection → pinning → velocity from the position change. If the rope works, the cloth is "just" the same thing in 2-D with more energy terms.
import torch
n, L = 21, 1.0
h = L / (n - 1)                                   # rest length of each segment
x = torch.stack([torch.linspace(0, 0.8, n), torch.zeros(n)], 1)   # ends 0.8 m apart -> it must sag
rest = x.clone(); v = torch.zeros_like(x); m, g, dt = 0.01, 9.81, 2e-3
pinned = torch.zeros(n, 1, dtype=torch.bool); pinned[0] = pinned[-1] = True
for step in range(4000):
    xr = x.detach().requires_grad_(True)
    E = (m * g * xr[:, 1]).sum()                  # gravity potential energy  m g y
    F = -torch.autograd.grad(E, xr)[0]            # force = -dE/dx
    v = 0.98 * v + dt * F / m                     # damped symplectic Euler
    xn = x + dt * v
    for _ in range(20):                           # PBD: make every segment length = h
        d = xn[1:] - xn[:-1]; Ls = d.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)
        c = 0.5 * (Ls - h) / Ls * d
        corr = torch.zeros_like(xn); corr[:-1] += c; corr[1:] -= c
        xn = torch.where(pinned, rest, xn + corr)
    v = (xn - x) / dt; x = xn
print("lowest point y = %.3f m" % x[:, 1].min().item())

Output: lowest point y = −0.266 m. Every segment length is 0.0500 (the target h). A 1 m rope with ends 0.8 m apart sags about 0.27 m, which is the correct catenary sag. ✔

The PBD distance correction, derived. Two points a, b should be distance h apart. Current vector \(d=b-a\), length \(L=|d|\). The constraint is \(C=L-h=0\). Moving both points equally (equal masses) along the line between them by half the error each gives:

$$a \leftarrow a + \tfrac12\frac{L-h}{L}\,d,\qquad b \leftarrow b - \tfrac12\frac{L-h}{L}\,d$$

Check: the new distance is \(L - (L-h) = h\). ✔ This is the gradient step \(\Delta x = -\frac{C}{|\nabla C|^2}\nabla C\) from Müller et al. (2007), with \(\nabla_a C = -d/L\) and \(\nabla_b C = d/L\).

Why PBD for stretch instead of a stretch energy? Real fabric is almost inextensible: its tensile stiffness is about 1000× its bending stiffness. A spring that stiff would need a tiny Δt (§5.4: \(\Delta t \le 2/\omega\), with \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\)). PBD enforces the length geometrically after each step, so it never enters the stability limit. Δt is then set only by the much softer bending and shear. This one choice made the simulator about 30× faster.
Run the rope. Then (1) change the 20 PBD iterations to 2 and watch the rope stretch, (2) remove the damping (0.98 → 1.0) and watch it swing forever, (3) move the ends to 1.0 m apart and check that the sag goes to 0.

Milestone B: the 2-D grid, masks and bookkeeping

A circle can't be meshed as a perfect square grid. The two options were (a) a triangle mesh of the circle, which needs a mesher and irregular indexing (slow loops, hard to batch), or (b) a square grid with a mask: make a full 41×41 grid and switch off the particles outside the circle. Option (b) turns every operation into array slicing like x[:,1:] - x[:,:-1], which is fast, batchable and differentiable. The cost is a slightly staircase edge, which is irrelevant at 7.5 mm spacing.
lin = torch.linspace(-R_SPEC, R_SPEC, n)          # n = 41 -> h = 0.30/40 = 7.5 mm
X, Y = torch.meshgrid(lin, lin, indexing="ij")    # X = warp direction, Y = weft direction
r = torch.sqrt(X**2 + Y**2)
inside = r <= R_SPEC + 1e-6                       # 1257 particles exist
pinned = r <= R_DISC                              # 441 are clamped by the disc
rest = torch.stack([X, Y, torch.zeros_like(X)], -1)   # (41,41,3) flat starting sheet
ex = inside[1:, :] & inside[:-1, :]               # edge along x exists if BOTH ends exist
ey = inside[:, 1:] & inside[:, :-1]
bx = inside[2:, :] & inside[1:-1, :] & inside[:-2, :]  # bending hinge needs 3 particles
by = inside[:, 2:] & inside[:, 1:-1] & inside[:, :-2]
q  = inside[1:,1:] & inside[:-1,1:] & inside[1:,:-1] & inside[:-1,:-1]  # shear quad needs 4

Why the shapes work. If x has shape (41, …), then x[1:] and x[:-1] both have shape (40, …), and element i of their difference is \(x_{i+1}-x_i\): all edges at once. Similarly x[2:] - 2*x[1:-1] + x[:-2] has shape (39, …) and gives \(x_{i+1}-2x_i+x_{i-1}\) for every interior i: all hinges at once. The masks have exactly these shapes, so multiplying by them switches off terms that touch a non-existent particle.

Expected counts: about \(\pi(20)^2 \approx 1257\) grid points inside a circle of radius 20 cells, and \(\pi(12)^2\approx 452\) (441 exactly) under the disc. 1257 − 441 = 816 free particles. The bending hinges number 1177 along x, and there are 1176 quads.

Print inside.sum(), pinned.sum(), bx.sum(), q.sum() and plot inside with plt.imshow. Getting these counts right is your first unit test.
Q: Why 41×41? h = 7.5 mm is fine enough to resolve folds: a fold wavelength is about 5–8 cm, so 7–10 particles per fold. A finer grid (81×81) makes each step 4× costlier and needs a Δt about 4× smaller (§5.4), so roughly 16× slower overall. That would have pushed dataset generation from 1 hour to about 16 hours on a laptop.

Milestone C: gravity and pinning

Each particle represents a patch of area \(h^2\). Its weight is \(w h^2\) (N), so its mass is \(m = w h^2 / g\). The gravity energy is

$$E_g=\sum_i (w h^2)\,z_i \;\Rightarrow\; F_{z,i} = -\frac{\partial E_g}{\partial z_i} = -w h^2 \;\Rightarrow\; a_z = F/m = -g\ ✔$$
Eg = w * h**2 * (x[..., 2] * inside).sum((1, 2))
mass = (w * h**2 / 9.81)[:, None, None, None]
...
xn = torch.where(pinned[None, ..., None], rest.expand_as(xn), xn)   # clamp disc region
Pinning is done by overwriting the pinned particles with their rest positions after every step. This is simpler and more robust than adding constraint forces. The force is also multiplied by free (zero on pinned particles), so they never get velocity.
With gravity only, the free ring falls straight down at g and the sheet visibly tears away from the disc. That shows you why you need Milestone D.

Milestone D: inextensibility in 2-D (Jacobi PBD)

This is the rope projection applied to x-edges and y-edges simultaneously:

for _ in range(iters):
    corr = zeros_like(x); cnt = zeros_like(x[..., :1])
    for dim, m in ((1, ex), (2, ey)):              # dim 1 = x-edges, dim 2 = y-edges
        a = x.narrow(dim, 0, N-1); b = x.narrow(dim, 1, N-1)
        d = b - a; L = d.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True) + 1e-12
        c = (L - h) / L * d * 0.5 * m[None, ..., None]
        corr.narrow(dim, 0, N-1).add_(c);  corr.narrow(dim, 1, N-1).sub_(c)
        cnt.narrow(dim, 0, N-1).add_(m);   cnt.narrow(dim, 1, N-1).add_(m)
    x = x + 1.5 * corr / cnt.clamp(min=1) * free

Gauss–Seidel vs Jacobi. Classic PBD (Gauss–Seidel) fixes one constraint at a time and updates immediately. It converges fast but is a sequential Python loop, which is very slow. Jacobi computes all corrections from the same old positions, adds them up, and averages by the number of constraints touching each particle (cnt, up to 4). It is fully parallel. On its own, averaging is too timid (it converges slowly), so we over-relax by ω = 1.5: \(x \leftarrow x + \omega\,\overline{\Delta x}\). Values of 1 < ω < 2 speed convergence. Above about 2 it oscillates.

narrow(dim, start, length) is a slice along a chosen dimension. It lets one loop body handle both directions. +1e-12 avoids dividing by zero.

Why only 8 iterations? PBD doesn't have to converge fully within one step, because leftover error gets corrected over the next steps. 8 gave edge-length errors under 1% at equilibrium. The fit (§9) uses 6 to save memory, since every iteration is stored for backprop.
Q: Is PBD physically accurate? PBD's effective stiffness depends on the iteration count and Δt (XPBD fixes this). We use it only to approximate an inextensible constraint, where "infinitely stiff" is the intended behaviour, so this dependence doesn't matter. Bending and shear, the properties we want to infer, are proper energies with physical units. That separation was deliberate.

Milestone E: anisotropic bending

From §5.2, the curvature at hinge i along x is \(\kappa \approx |x_{i+1}-2x_i+x_{i-1}|/h^2\). The energy is the energy density × area:

$$E_b=\sum_{\text{x-hinges}}\tfrac12 B_1\kappa^2 h^2 = \frac{B_1}{2h^2}\sum|\Delta_x^2 x|^2,\qquad\text{same with } B_2 \text{ along } y$$ 
d2x = x[:, 2:] - 2*x[:, 1:-1] + x[:, :-2]          # (b,39,41,3)
d2y = x[:, :, 2:] - 2*x[:, :, 1:-1] + x[:, :, :-2]  # (b,41,39,3)
Eb = 0.5*B1*((d2x**2).sum(-1)*bx).sum((1,2))/h**2 + 0.5*B2*((d2y**2).sum(-1)*by).sum((1,2))/h**2
Because d2x is a 3-D vector difference, this measures curvature in any direction (up–down and sideways), not only vertical sag. It is the simplest discrete bending model. Real simulators use dihedral-angle bending on triangles (Bridson 2003), but on a regular grid and with an inextensible sheet the second difference is equivalent and far simpler. Separate B1 (warp, axis x) and B2 (weft, axis y) is exactly how KES reports bending.
Run with B1 = B2 at 1e-6, 1e-5 and 1e-4 and print the DC. It must rise monotonically. Then set B1 = 10·B2: the drape should become oval, sticking out further along x (the stiffer warp direction resists bending there).

Milestone F: shear

For each quad, take the two edges leaving its corner: \(e_1=x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,j}\) and \(e_2=x_{i,j+1}-x_{i,j}\). Undeformed, they are at 90°. Under shear angle γ, the angle becomes \(90°-\gamma\), so

$$\cos\theta=\frac{e_1\cdot e_2}{|e_1||e_2|}=\cos(90°-\gamma)=\sin\gamma\approx\gamma$$ $$E_s=\sum_{\text{quads}}\tfrac12 G\gamma^2 h^2\approx \tfrac12 G h^2\sum\cos^2\theta$$

Units: G is in N/m (force per width per radian), so G·γ²·h² is in N·m = J. ✔

e1 = x[:, 1:, :-1] - x[:, :-1, :-1]
e2 = x[:, :-1, 1:] - x[:, :-1, :-1]
cos = (e1*e2).sum(-1) / (e1.norm(dim=-1)*e2.norm(dim=-1) + 1e-12)
Es = 0.5 * G * ((cos**2) * q).sum((1,2)) * h**2
In this model G is an "effective" shear parameter. Its range (0.05–5 N/m) was chosen so that shear visibly changes the folds without freezing the sheet. It is not the KES G (which is in gf/cm·deg). Say this honestly in interviews. Mapping it to KES values would need calibration against real measured fabrics, which is listed as future work.

Milestone G: the pedestal collision

rxy = x[..., :2].norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
below = x[..., 2:3] < -0.002                  # more than 2 mm below the disc plane
push = below & (rxy < 0.98 * R_DISC)          # ... and inside the support cylinder
scale = torch.where(push, 0.98*R_DISC/(rxy+1e-9), 1)
x = cat([x[..., :2]*scale, x[..., 2:3]], -1)  # push radially out to the cylinder wall
Without this, very limp fabric folded inward under the disc, which is impossible in a real tester because there is a support column there. The fix is a simple projection: any particle below the disc plane and inside the column radius is pushed radially back to the column surface. It is the same idea as PBD (fix positions geometrically). The 2 mm tolerance avoids fighting with particles at the disc edge.

Milestone H: the per-sample time step (a real bug story)

Symptom: in the first dataset test, many soft fabrics had DC stuck at 0.71–0.82, close to "stiff". They looked half-fallen.
Diagnosis: I used one global Δt chosen for the stiffest fabric. Soft, light fabrics then moved very little per step and hadn't reached equilibrium after 2500 steps. I checked by running them for 4× as many steps: DC kept dropping.
Fix: give each sample its own stable Δt.
$$\Delta t_i = 0.9\sqrt{\frac{m_i}{k_{\max,i}}},\qquad k_{\max}=\frac{16\max(B_1,B_2)}{h^2}+4G,\qquad \Delta t_i\le 4\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}$$

(From §5.4: explicit stability needs \(\Delta t < 2/\omega=2\sqrt{m/k}\). Using 0.9√(m/k) leaves a safety factor of about 2.) The cap of 4 ms stops very soft samples from taking steps so large that PBD and collisions misbehave.

kmax = 16*torch.maximum(B1, B2)/h**2 + 4*G
dt = (0.9*torch.sqrt(mass.flatten()/kmax)).clamp(max=4e-3)[:, None, None, None]

Because dt has shape (b,1,1,1), it broadcasts, so every fabric in the batch advances with its own step size within the same tensor operation.

Milestone I: damping and reaching equilibrium

We want the static drape: the minimum of \(E_b+E_s+E_g\) subject to the constraints. Two ways to get it: (1) direct energy minimisation, or (2) damped dynamics, which simulates and lets friction-like damping bleed off kinetic energy until it stops. I used (2) because the PBD constraints and the collision fit naturally into a time-stepper.

$$v\leftarrow(1-c)\,v+\Delta t\,F/m,\qquad c=0.03$$

Each step removes 3% of the velocity: after n steps it is scaled by \(0.97^n\). After 100 steps that is 0.05, so oscillations die quickly. Too much damping (for example 0.3) makes motion "syrupy" and slow to settle. Too little and it keeps ringing.

After the projection, v = (xn - x)/dt: the velocity is recomputed from the actual motion, including the constraint corrections. This is standard in PBD. Without it, the velocity would keep pushing against constraints.

Convergence test: simulate the same 32 fabrics with 2000 and with 4000 steps. Mean |ΔDC| = 0.008, against a dataset DC spread of about 0.6. The answer had converged. I used 2500 steps for the dataset. Very high G (> 5) converged noticeably slower, which is one reason G is capped at 5.

Milestone J: breaking symmetry

A perfectly flat, perfectly symmetric sheet under perfectly symmetric gravity has no reason to choose where to fold. In exact arithmetic it would sink as an axisymmetric cone. Real fabric always has tiny imperfections. I added 1 mm of random vertical noise to the free particles at the start. Folds then appear naturally (a buckling instability), just as in a real test. The noise is seeded, so every dataset sample is reproducible.
gen = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
x[..., 2] += 1e-3 * torch.randn(x.shape[:-1], generator=gen) * free[..., 0]
Q: Doesn't random noise make the label noisy? Partly, and that is realistic: repeated real drape tests of the same fabric give different node positions and DC that varies by about ±0.02. The CNN must learn features that are robust to this, such as fold count and amplitude rather than exact fold angles. That is one reason the flip augmentation is safe.

Milestone K: batching (32 fabrics at once)

Every tensor gets a leading batch dimension: positions are (b, 41, 41, 3), and parameters are (b,). Per-sample scalars are reshaped so they broadcast:

  • B1 (b,) multiplies a per-sample sum of shape (b,) directly.
  • mass[:, None, None, None] → (b,1,1,1), dividing a force of shape (b,41,41,3).

Broadcasting rule: align shapes from the right. Dimensions of size 1 are stretched to match. A batch of 32 costs about the same wall time as 4–8 single runs, because the CPU uses vector instructions on larger arrays.

The energy function returns one energy per sample (b,). We call .sum() before autograd. Since sample i's energy depends only on sample i's positions, \(\partial(\sum_j E_j)/\partial x_i = \partial E_i/\partial x_i\). One backward pass gives every sample's own force (see §5.3).

Surprise: Apple's MPS GPU was slower than the CPU for the simulator. Each step launches many small kernels (slices, norms, the PBD loop), and the GPU launch overhead dominates when tensors are only 32×41×41×3. GPUs win on big, dense operations like CNN convolutions, so training used MPS while the simulator stayed on the CPU. Lesson: measure, don't assume.

Milestone L: measurements (DC, nodes, silhouette)

Drape coefficient from the polar outline

Convert free-particle positions to polar coordinates (r, θ). Split θ into 180 bins of 2°. In each bin, take the maximum radius \(r_{\max}(\theta)\): this is the edge of the shadow. The shadow area of a star-shaped region is

$$A=\int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac12 r(\theta)^2\,d\theta\approx\tfrac12\sum_k r_k^2\,\Delta\theta,\qquad \Delta\theta=\frac{2\pi}{180}$$

(the area of a thin wedge of radius r and angle dθ is ½r²dθ). Clamp \(r_k\ge R_{disc}\), because the disc itself always casts shadow. Then apply the Cusick formula from §4.1.

bins = ((th + pi)/(2*pi)*180).long().clamp(0, 179)
rmax = zeros(b, 180).scatter_reduce(1, bins, r, "amax", include_self=True)   # per-bin max
rmax = maximum(rmax, R_DISC)
area = 0.5*(rmax**2).sum(1)*(2*pi/180)

scatter_reduce(..., "amax") is a vectorised "group-by bin, take max". It avoids a Python loop over particles.

Node count

Smooth \(r(\theta)\) three times with the binomial kernel [1,4,6,4,1]/16 (an approximation of a Gaussian). Pad it circularly (concatenate the last 2 and first 2 values), because θ wraps around. Then count local maxima that stand at least 2 mm above the mean radius. Smoothing removes jagged peaks caused by individual particles. The prominence threshold removes tiny bumps. Result: 5–12 nodes, which is the real range (typical fabrics show 5–10).

Silhouette image (the CNN's input)

  1. Splat: map each particle's (x, y) from [−0.15, 0.15] m to pixel indices 0..63, and set those pixels to 1 (scatter_).
  2. Closing (dilate then erode, done with max-pooling): fills the 1-pixel gaps between particles. Dilation = max_pool2d(img,3,1,1). Erosion = -max_pool2d(-img,…), because min(x) = −max(−x).
  3. Fill holes: flood-fill the background starting from the image border (repeatedly dilate it, but only into empty pixels). Anything not reached is inside the shape, so set it to 1. This is the same trick used in scipy.ndimage.binary_fill_holes, written here in PyTorch.
Why a binary silhouette and not a 3-D height map? Because the Cusick test and a phone photo from above give you a shadow/outline, not depth. Using only what the real measurement provides keeps the sim-to-real path open (§10). The height map is still saved in the dataset for future work.

Milestone V: validating the simulator

  • Monotonicity: DC increases with B/w. The dataset figure (DC vs log(√(B1B2)/w)) shows a tight monotonic band, as Cusick's experiments predict. In fact, DC is known empirically to correlate with bending length.
  • Range: DC spans about 0.2–0.95, matching real fabrics from chiffon to canvas.
  • Nodes: 5–12. Stiffer fabrics give fewer, larger folds, which matches the literature.
  • Anisotropy: B1 ≠ B2 produces elongated drapes, as with real woven fabric.
  • Convergence: 2000 vs 4000 steps gives ΔDC 0.008 (Milestone I).
  • Constraints: edge lengths within ~1% of h.
  • Visual: figures/example_drapes.png (below).
Q: How do you know the simulator is right? "I validated it at three levels. Numerically: constraint error, convergence in steps. Physically: monotonic DC vs bending length, realistic DC and node-count ranges, anisotropy effects. And against first principles: the rope matched catenary sag. What I have not done is quantitative validation against real measured fabrics, and that's the first thing I'd do with lab access."

7. Phase 5: Generating the dataset

Connects: calls the simulator (§6) thousands of times with random fabrics → saves (silhouette, w) as input and (B1, B2, G) as labels → feeds the CNN (§8).

7.1 What fabrics to sample, and why log-uniform

$$\log_{10}B_1\sim U(-6,-4),\quad B_2=B_1\cdot10^{U(-0.5,0.5)},\quad \log_{10}G\sim U(-1.3,0.7),\quad w\sim U(0.6,3)\ \text{N/m}^2$$

Log-uniform means every decade is equally likely: as many fabrics in 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁵ as in 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴. If you sampled B uniformly in [10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁴], 90% of samples would fall in the top decade, and limp silks would barely appear. Stiffness varies by orders of magnitude, so the natural scale is logarithmic. That is also why the CNN predicts log₁₀ B (§8.2).

Anisotropy \(B_2/B_1\in[0.32, 3.2]\) covers real woven fabrics (commonly 0.5–2). w = 0.6–3 N/m² is about 60–300 GSM: voile to denim.

8. Phase 6: The CNN (learning the inverse)

Connects: input = silhouette (Milestone L) + w (known by weighing). Output = log B1, log B2, log G (the simulator's inputs). It is an amortised inverse of §6, and in §9 its prediction becomes the starting point for physics fitting.

Convolution. \(y_{o}(i,j)=b_o+\sum_{c}\sum_{u,v=-1}^{1}W_{o,c,u,v}\;x_c(i+u,j+v)\). The same 3×3 filter slides over the whole image (weight sharing + translation equivariance). Parameters: \(o\cdot c\cdot 9+o\).

Output size: \(\;H_{out}=\left\lfloor\frac{H+2p-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1\). With k = 3, p = 1, s = 1: \(H_{out}=H\) ("same" padding). MaxPool k = 2, s = 2 halves it: 64→32→16→8→4.

BatchNorm: \(\hat x=\frac{x-\mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2+\epsilon}},\ y=\gamma\hat x+\beta\), per channel over the batch. It keeps activations well-scaled, allows a higher learning rate and acts as a mild regulariser. At test time (m.eval()), it uses running averages instead of batch statistics. Forgetting eval() is a classic bug.

GELU: \(\text{GELU}(x)=x\,\Phi(x)\), where Φ is the standard normal CDF. It is a smooth ReLU. ReLU would work almost the same here.

Global average pooling: averages each channel over all positions → one number per channel. It is far fewer parameters than flattening, and it makes the model care about what features are present (folds, their sharpness) more than exactly where.

Parameter count (check it yourself): conv 1→32: 32·1·9+32 = 320; 32→32: 9,248; 32→64: 18,496; 64→64: 36,928; 64→128: 73,856; 128→128: 147,584 (×3 in blocks 3–4); BN: 2 per channel; head: 129·128+128 + 128·3+3 = 17,027. Total = 600,035 (printed by PyTorch).

Receptive field after 4 blocks: each 3×3 conv adds 2·(current stride) pixels. This gives roughly 76 pixels, more than the 64-pixel image, so the final features "see" the whole drape.

Design reasoning. This is a standard VGG-style CNN: small, proven and easy to explain. I did not use a pretrained ResNet, because the input is 1-channel binary shapes, not natural photos, so ImageNet features don't transfer well, and the model would be 20× bigger. The input w is concatenated after the convolutions: it's a scalar about the fabric and not a spatial thing. Physically, drape depends on B/w (bending length, §4.2 and §4.5), so without w the network could not separate "stiff and heavy" from "soft and light". Adding w as an input is physics-informed design.

8.3 Metrics and the baseline

$$R^2 = 1-\frac{\sum(\hat y-y)^2}{\sum(y-\bar y)^2}$$

R² is the fraction of variance explained. 1 is perfect. 0 is "no better than predicting the mean". It can be negative if worse than that.

MAE (log₁₀) is the mean of \(|\hat y-y|\). Median factor error is \(10^{\text{median}|\hat y-y|}\), e.g. 1.05 means a typical prediction is within 5%.

Baseline: the "industry" approach summarises a drape test by one number, DC. To be fair, I gave the baseline DC and w, with nonlinear features: \(\log B\approx a_1 DC + a_2 w + a_3 DC\cdot w+a_4DC^2+a_5\), fitted by least squares (torch.linalg.lstsq) on the same training split. If the CNN can't beat this, the image adds nothing.

9. Phase 7: Differentiable physics (the second inverse method)

Connects: reuses sim.run from §6 with grad=True, the autograd ideas from §5.3, and the CNN from §8 as the initial guess. It is "analysis-by-synthesis": find parameters whose simulated drape matches the observed one.

9.1 The idea

$$p^*=\arg\min_{p}\ \mathcal L(p),\quad \mathcal L(p)=10^4\cdot\frac1{90}\sum_{k}\big(\tilde r_k(\text{sim}(10^{p},w))-r_k^{\text{target}}\big)^2,\quad p=(\log_{10}B_1,\log_{10}B_2,\log_{10}G)$$

Gradient descent needs \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial p\). The simulator is a chain of 600 steps \(x_{t+1}=\Phi(x_t,p)\), so by the chain rule:

$$\frac{d\mathcal L}{dp}=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_T}\sum_{t}\Big(\prod_{s>t}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_s}\Big)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p}\Big|_t$$

PyTorch computes this automatically by recording every operation in all 600 steps and running backward once. This is backpropagation through time (BPTT), the same thing used for RNNs.

9.2 The two technical tricks

(1) Second-order graph. Inside each step, the force is itself a gradient: \(F=-\nabla_x E(x,p)\). To differentiate the loss with respect to p we need derivatives of F, i.e. second derivatives of E. torch.autograd.grad(E, xr, create_graph=True) keeps the graph of the gradient computation so that it can be differentiated again. Without create_graph, the forces would be constants and \(\partial F/\partial B=0\), so the gradients would be wrong.

(2) A differentiable outline. The DC/outline in Milestone L uses max and integer binning, whose gradients are zero almost everywhere. I replaced them with a soft-max:

$$\tilde r_k=\frac1\tau\log\frac{\sum_i w_{ik}\,e^{\tau r_i}}{\sum_i w_{ik}},\qquad w_{ik}=\exp\!\Big(-\big(\tfrac{\theta_i-c_k}{2\pi/90}\big)^2\Big),\ \tau=200$$

As τ→∞, this becomes the max over the particles near angle \(c_k\). With τ = 200 m⁻¹, a radius difference of 1 cm changes the weight by \(e^2\approx 7\), so it's close to a max but smooth. The Gaussian weights replace hard bins with soft angular membership. In code, torch.logsumexp computes \(\log\sum e^{a_i}\) stably by subtracting the maximum first, avoiding overflow from e^(200·0.15) = e^30.

The hybrid ran 25 iterations × 1200 steps at lr 0.03 in about 56 s (python -m drapenet.fit_physics --cnn_init --iters 25 --steps 1200 --lr 0.03).

Why the naive start failed: the loss landscape is non-convex. Raising warp bending and raising shear both make the outline stiffer, so the optimiser found a "valley" where too-low B1 is compensated by too-high G. This is the ill-posedness of §3 in action. Why the hybrid works: the CNN gets into the right basin instantly (it has seen thousands of examples), and the physics fit then refines locally using the actual equations, with no generalisation error. This combination of learned prior + physics refinement is a recurring pattern in scientific ML, and it's a strong interview story.
Q: Cost of differentiable simulation? Memory grows linearly with the number of steps, because every intermediate is stored for backward: about 0.8 GB for 600 steps. Fixes: gradient checkpointing (recompute instead of store), fewer steps, or implicit differentiation at equilibrium (adjoint method, where you differentiate the condition ∇E = 0 instead of the trajectory). Long backprop chains can also give exploding or vanishing gradients, which damping helps keep in check.

10. Phase 8: Towards real fabrics (sim-to-real)

10.1 From a photo to a silhouette (predict_real.py)

  1. Greyscale + threshold: threshold = midpoint of the 5th and 95th percentile intensities. This is robust to a few very bright or dark pixels, and a simple stand-in for Otsu's method. Use --invert if the fabric is darker than the background.
  2. Centre: centroid of the foreground pixels.
  3. Scale: crop a square of ±R_SPEC·px_per_m around the centroid, so the image spans 30 cm, the same physical window as the simulator. Then resize to 64×64.
  4. Fill holes with the same function as the simulator, so preprocessing is identical in sim and real.
  5. Coordinate transform: image row index points down = −y, and column = x. The simulator array is indexed [x, y] with y up. So t.flip(0).T: flip rows (y down → y up), then transpose to [x, y]. Getting this wrong would swap warp and weft.
  6. Predict: GSM → w = GSM·10⁻³·9.81, standardise with the saved wmu/wsd, run the CNN, un-standardise, take 10^. Report B in N·m and gf·cm²/cm, and the bending length \(C=(B/w)^{1/3}\).
Round-trip test: render a synthetic drape, save it as a PNG, run predict_real.py on it, and check that it recovers the known B. ✔ This verifies the whole image pipeline, including the coordinate transform, without needing real fabric.

10.2 A home drape rig (no textile hardware)

  • 18 cm plate or tin lid on a 25 cm stand (a jar or a stack of books); 30 cm fabric circles (draw with string and a pencil, mark warp with an arrow).
  • Phone directly above (check with a level app), dark background, warp arrow to the right of the image, a ruler in frame for px_per_m.
  • Weigh each circle on a kitchen scale: GSM = grams / (π·0.15²).
  • Validation: do the Peirce cantilever test (§4.2) on strips of the same fabrics with a ruler and a 41.5° angle guide. Compare the predicted bending length with the measured one using Spearman rank correlation (does the model at least order fabrics correctly?). That is an honest first sim-to-real test with 10–20 fabrics.
  • Arduino option: an HC-SR04 or VL53L0X distance sensor, or a servo-driven cantilever with a tilt sensor (MPU6050) to automate reading the 41.5° angle. This makes the cantilever measurement repeatable and adds hardware/automation to your CV.
Q: What's the sim-to-real gap and how would you close it? "Sources: real fabric has friction against the disc, hysteresis and non-linear bending, plus lighting and camera perspective. Mitigations: (1) domain randomisation: add noise, blur, slight scale/rotation and threshold errors during training; (2) calibrate the simulator's parameters against a few fabrics with KES data; (3) fine-tune on a small real labelled set; (4) use the physics fit (§9) directly on the real outline, since it has no learned component to mismatch."

14. Interview question bank (with model answers)

Try answering each one out loud before expanding it.

The big picture

1. Explain the project in 30 seconds.

"Measuring fabric bending and shear needs expensive instruments like KES-F. I built a differentiable cloth simulator of the standard Cusick drape test in PyTorch, generated 3,000 virtual fabrics, and trained a CNN to predict warp bending, weft bending and shear from just the top-view drape silhouette plus fabric weight. It reaches R² 0.99 for bending and 0.97 for shear, while the industry-standard drape coefficient explains essentially none of the shear. I also invert the simulator directly with gradient descent, and combining the two (CNN guess, then physics refinement) recovers parameters within 2%."

2. What's novel here?

The question "what does the full drape shape say beyond DC?", answered quantitatively with a baseline. Plus the hybrid learned + differentiable-physics inverse in a textile setting, with everything running on a laptop. I'm not claiming the simulator is new: it uses standard PBD + energy methods.

3. Why should a textile engineer care?

Fast, cheap fabric characterisation: from a phone photo for QC, e-commerce (virtual try-on needs these parameters), and digital fabric twins for 3-D garment design (CLO3D/Browzwear need bending and shear inputs).

4. What are the limitations?

Simulated data only; G is an effective parameter, not KES-calibrated; no friction or hysteresis; coarse grid; real-photo validation not yet done; the CNN gives no uncertainty estimate.

Physics and textiles

5. Define drape coefficient. What does DC = 0.3 mean?

(A_shadow − A_disc)/(A_spec − A_disc). At 0.3, the shadow covers 30% of the annulus, so it is a fairly limp fabric (like a light viscose).

6. What is bending rigidity, and what are its units?

Moment per unit width per unit curvature, M = Bκ, in N·m. Energy density ½Bκ². In KES units, gf·cm²/cm (1 = 9.81×10⁻⁵ N·m).

7. What is bending length, and how is it related to B?

C = (B/w)^{1/3}, from the Peirce cantilever test (overhang at 41.5°, C = l/2). It is the length scale on which bending beats gravity.

8. Why does shear affect drape?

Folding a disc into a draped shape needs double curvature, which an inextensible sheet can only get by shearing. Shear stiffness changes the number and roundness of folds, but barely changes the projected area.

9. Why model stretch as a constraint?

Fabric tensile stiffness is about 1000× larger. As an energy it would force a tiny Δt. PBD enforces near-inextensibility geometrically, at no stability cost.

10. How did you discretise bending?

Second differences on the grid: κ ≈ |x_{i+1} − 2x_i + x_{i−1}|/h², E = (B/2h²)Σ|Δ²x|², with separate B along x (warp) and y (weft).

11. Why do folds form at all?

Buckling: the circumference of the hanging ring has to shrink as it falls, and compressing an inextensible sheet makes it buckle into waves. The wavelength is set by the balance of bending vs gravity (and shear). Seeded noise picks where the folds appear.

12. How does weight enter, and why is w an input?

The drape depends on B/w (dimensionless group B/(wR³)). Without w, "stiff and heavy" looks like "soft and light". Weight is easy to measure, so we give it to the model.

Numerics

13. Explicit vs implicit integration?

Explicit is cheap per step but conditionally stable (Δt < 2/ω). Implicit (Baraff–Witkin) is stable for big steps but needs a linear solve every step. We only need equilibrium, so explicit + damping + PBD was simplest and easy to batch and differentiate.

14. How did you choose Δt?

Δt = 0.9√(m/k_max) per sample, with k_max ≈ 16B/h² + 4G, capped at 4 ms. A global Δt left soft fabrics unconverged.

15. How do you know it's at equilibrium?

Convergence test: 2000 vs 4000 steps gives mean ΔDC of 0.008, far below the dataset spread.

16. What's symplectic Euler, and why use it?

Update v first using the force at x, then x using the new v. It conserves energy much better than forward Euler, which steadily gains energy and explodes. That makes it the standard choice for particle physics in games and graphics.

17. Jacobi vs Gauss–Seidel PBD?

GS updates sequentially and converges faster, but can't be vectorised. Jacobi computes all corrections in parallel and averages them. I over-relax by 1.5 to compensate for its slower convergence.

18. Why did the GPU lose to the CPU?

Tiny tensors and many small ops: kernel-launch overhead dominates. CNN training (big convolutions) was faster on MPS.

Machine learning

19. Why a CNN?

The input is an image whose informative features are local shapes (folds) that can appear anywhere. Convolutions share weights and are translation-equivariant, so they need far fewer parameters than an MLP.

20. Walk through the architecture and its shapes.

1×64×64 → 32×32×32 → 64×16×16 → 128×8×8 → 128×4×4 → GAP 128 → +w = 129 → 128 → 3. About 600k parameters.

21. Why predict log values?

The parameters span 2 orders of magnitude. Log makes errors relative (a factor), balances the loss across scales and suits log-uniform sampling.

22. Why standardise the targets?

So the three outputs have similar scale and contribute equally to the loss. The statistics come from the training set only, to avoid leakage.

23. Why smooth L1 rather than MSE?

It is less sensitive to a few ambiguous samples with big errors. It behaves like MSE near zero.

24. Which augmentations, and why not rotations?

Flips keep warp on the x axis, so the label is unchanged. A 90° rotation swaps warp and weft, so the label would have to be swapped too.

25. How did you avoid overfitting?

Validation-based checkpointing, weight decay, dropout, BN, augmentation and a held-out test set used once.

26. What does BatchNorm do, and its train/eval difference?

It normalises per channel using batch statistics in training and running statistics in eval. Forgetting model.eval() gives wrong test predictions.

27. Explain R², and why include a baseline?

R² is the fraction of variance explained. The baseline shows the value the image adds over the existing single-number method. Without it, 0.99 means nothing.

28. Why is shear harder (0.97) than bending (0.995)?

Shear leaves a subtler signature (fold rounding and count, not area), and it partly trades off with bending. The model relies on finer features.

29. How would you add uncertainty?

Deep ensembles, MC dropout, a Gaussian output head (predict mean and variance, NLL loss), or simulation-based inference with normalising flows to get a full posterior. That would expose the B–G trade-off directly.

30. Would a transformer help?

A ViT needs much more data. A CNN is the right inductive bias at 3k images. A transformer or GNN would make sense on the raw particle mesh (e.g. MeshGraphNets) or to learn a fast surrogate simulator.

Differentiable physics

31. How do you backprop through a simulator?

All operations are PyTorch ops, so autograd records 600+ steps and backprop goes through time. The forces are gradients themselves, so create_graph=True gives the needed second derivatives.

32. Why a soft outline?

max and binning have zero or undefined gradients. A logsumexp soft-max with Gaussian angular weights is smooth, and approaches the max as τ grows.

33. Why did the naive fit fail?

Non-convex loss and a B1–G trade-off valley (ill-posedness). The CNN initialisation lands in the right basin.

34. CNN vs physics fitting: which is better?

The CNN is instant (amortised) but limited by training data and generalisation. Fitting is slow (~1 min) but uses the exact physics and works on any single case. The hybrid gets the best of both.

35. Memory cost, and how to reduce it?

Linear in the number of steps (~0.8 GB for 600). Use checkpointing, fewer steps, or the adjoint/implicit-function theorem at equilibrium.

Real world and next steps

36. How would you test on real fabric?

Home drape rig + phone, GSM by weighing, cantilever bending length as ground truth, Spearman correlation over 10–20 fabrics. Later, KES data from a lab.

37. Biggest sim-to-real risks?

Friction, non-linear and hysteretic bending, the uncalibrated shear parameter, camera perspective and thresholding. Mitigations are in §10.

38. If you had 3 more months?

Real validation dataset, calibration to KES, a finer mesh or GNN surrogate, uncertainty quantification, side-view images for more information, and knitted fabrics.

39. What would you do differently?

Write the convergence test and per-sample Δt from day one, use a proper triangle mesh with dihedral bending for accuracy, and log experiments (e.g. Weights & Biases).

40. What did you learn?

Turning physics into code (energies → forces), numerical stability, careful ML evaluation with baselines, and that the hardest part of scientific ML is making sure the data generator is right.
